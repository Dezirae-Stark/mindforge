# Seiðr — Citation Record

Bibliographic records for `seidr.html`'s research basis, per the Prior-Art Protocol in `CLAUDE.md`. Every entry below was verified during the citation audit of 2026-08-05 / 2026-08-08 against Crossref, PubMed, or the publisher of record. Nothing here is cited from memory.

**Verification key:** ✅ verified against Crossref/PubMed · 📄 verified against publisher or archive copy · ⚠️ exists but the finding is disputed or failed replication · ❌ claim not supported by any source found

## Primary source for the practice

- 📄 **Eiríks saga rauða**, ch. 4 — the Þorbjörg lítilvölva episode. The single most-cited textual source for a seiðr performance: the seeress on the high seat (*seiðhjallr*), the assembled household, and the song **varðlokkur** sung to draw the spirits before prophecy is given. This is the passage the tool's ceremonial structure reconstructs.

## Scholarship — read all three, they disagree

- 📄 **Tolley, C. (2009).** *Shamanism in Norse Myth and Magic*, 2 vols. Folklore Fellows' Communications 296–297. Academia Scientiarum Fennica, Helsinki.
  The most exhaustive treatment. **Tolley's conclusion cuts against this tool's framing:** he finds the sources too late and too literarily mediated to establish classic shamanism in Norse tradition, and argues for broader Indo-European influence instead. Cite him honestly — he is the strongest scholarly voice *against* reading seiðr as shamanism.

- 📄 **Price, N. (2002/2019).** *The Viking Way: Magic and Mind in Late Iron Age Scandinavia.* Oxbow Books (2nd ed. 2019).
  The influential case *for* reading seiðr through a circumpolar/Sámi comparative lens; discusses the *seiðstafr* (staff) and the high seat as ritual apparatus.

- 📄 **Heide, E.** — work on *varðlokkur* and on seiðr as a spinning/attracting metaphor ("Spinning Seiðr"). The etymology the tool relies on — *vǫrðr* (warden/guardian spirit) + a calling/enclosing element, hence a song to lure warden spirits — comes from this line of scholarship.
  ⚠️ Specific volume and page references for Heide have **not** been pinned down and are not recorded here. Do not cite a page number for Heide until one is verified.

## Auditory driving (the drumming rationale)

- 📄 **Neher, A. (1962).** A physiological explanation of unusual behavior in ceremonies involving drums. *Human Biology*, 34(2), 151–160. PMID 14478805.
  No Crossref DOI. The origin of the "auditory driving" hypothesis and the source of the tool's 180–220 BPM range.
  ⚠️ **Neher's methodology has been criticised for decades** — laboratory conditions with electronic tone bursts bear little resemblance to a ceremony, and later work has not cleanly reproduced the claimed entrainment-to-trance link. Cite it as the origin of the hypothesis, not as its confirmation.

- ✅ **Flor-Henry, P., Shapiro, Y., & Sombrun, C. (2017).** Brain changes during a shamanic trance: altered modes of consciousness, hemispheric laterality, and systemic psychobiology. *Cogent Psychology*, 4(1), article 1313522. doi:[10.1080/23311908.2017.1313522](https://doi.org/10.1080/23311908.2017.1313522)
  ⚠️ **n = 1.** A single trained subject able to enter trance at will. Genuinely interesting, and not a basis for any general claim.

## Scope note

The varðlokkur text pre-loaded in the tool is a modern composition in Old Norse style, not a transmitted historical text — no varðlokkur lyrics survive. The Nine Worlds three-tier arrangement is a common modern schematisation; the medieval sources do not present a tidy nine-world cosmology. Both are presented as practice scaffolding and should not be described as reconstructions of attested material.
